First, look at where we look.

Records pile up where people live. Before reading any pattern in the birds, see where the observers are.

75.6%

of records come from Victoria, New South Wales and Queensland.

A map of sightings is also a map of people.

Canberra is small and city-bound. The Territory has few residents.

So each Territorian logs 3.6 times the national rate.

Turn the calendar. The cast changes.

Australia is in the southern hemisphere, so its seasons run opposite to the northern calendar: summer is December to February, winter is June to August.

October is the busiest month.

The tropical Northern Territory keeps its own clock, busiest in the dry season, June to October.

The Rainbow Bee-eater: a summer south, a winter north.

In summer, 48.3% of bee-eater records lie south of 30°S. In winter, just 1.0% do.

All records lean north in winter, so part of any shift is the birdwatchers. The bee-eater’s shift is far larger.

Often recorded doesn’t mean safe.

The Australian Government lists threatened species by their risk of extinction: Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered.

A record shows a bird was seen, not that it is recovering.

The Critically Endangered Eastern Curlew is the most logged, at 9,373 records.

Sources and method

Observation data

Atlas of Living Australia, hosting eBird Australia, BirdLife Australia’s Birdata and iNaturalist Australia. Downloaded 10 September 2026 as complete aggregate counts: records marked present, with a valid location and month, in the eight states and territories.

Conservation, people and land

DCCEEW Threatened Species State Lists, 28 August 2026. ABS estimated resident population, June 2024. Geoscience Australia land areas. Natural Earth boundaries. BirdLife Australia bee-eater profile.

Reading the evidence

Counts are database records, not birds or people. One sighting can be logged on more than one platform, and busy places are logged more often. Every map uses an equal-area (Albers) projection, so areas compare fairly.

Methods and limitations

9,575,922 records cover 760 named species; 217,108 more have no species name and count only in totals. Monthly shares divide a bird’s records by all records that month. The seasonal clock divides by days in the month and compares each state with its own average.

The contour map spreads each season’s 1° counts with a Gaussian kernel (σ = 1°), converts them to shares of that season’s total and contours the log ratio of winter to summer share on a 0.25° grid, at a constant interval of one doubling. It is an estimate; grey land has too few records.

Every chart uses 2024, the latest complete year. eBird supplies 86.8% of 2024 records, and this download holds no eBird records after August 2025.

Threatened species are matched to records by exact scientific name at species level: 65 match. A bird’s 2026 listing says nothing about its numbers in 2024.

Density uses Geoscience Australia land area and ABS June 2024 residents. The bee-eater map sums 1° squares into 2° squares so circles do not overplot. 1° squares shrink slightly towards the south.
