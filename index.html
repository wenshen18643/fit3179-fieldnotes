Australia, through a birdwatcher’s eyes

First, look at where we look.

Records pile up where people live. Before reading any pattern in the birds, see where the observers are.

Turn the calendar. The cast changes.

Australia is in the southern hemisphere, so its seasons run opposite to the northern calendar: summer is December to February, winter is June to August.

Often recorded doesn’t mean safe.

The Australian Government lists threatened species by their risk of extinction: Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered. A record shows that a bird was seen, not that it is doing well.

Records map the watchers, not just the birds.

9.6 million records show where people live, when they go out and which birds are easy to spot. They cannot say whether a bird is common or safe: the most-logged threatened bird is Critically Endangered. New records count most in the inland, the north and the quiet months. Malaysian birdwatchers can add to the same story: the Red-necked Stint, logged on Australian shores in summer, also feeds on Malaysian mudflats on the same flyway to and from Siberia.

Methods and limitations

Counts are database records, not birds or people. One sighting can be logged on more than one platform, and busy places are logged more often. Every map uses an equal-area (Albers) projection, so areas compare fairly.

9,575,922 records cover 760 named species; 217,108 more have no species name and count only in totals. Monthly shares divide a bird’s records by all records that month. The seasonal clock divides by days in the month and compares each state with its own average.

The contour map spreads each season’s 1° counts with a Gaussian kernel (σ = 1°), converts them to shares of that season’s total and contours the log ratio of winter to summer share on a 0.25° grid, at a constant interval of one doubling. It is an estimate; grey land has too few records.

Every chart uses 2024, the latest complete year. eBird supplies 86.8% of 2024 records, and this download holds no eBird records after August 2025.

Threatened species are matched to records by scientific name at species level: 69 match, including 4 recorded under newer names, such as Zanda for the black-cockatoos. A bird’s 2026 listing says nothing about its numbers in 2024.

Density uses Geoscience Australia land area and ABS June 2024 residents. The bee-eater map sums 1° squares into 2° squares so circles do not overplot. 1° squares shrink towards the south: one in Tasmania covers about a quarter less land than one at Cape York.
